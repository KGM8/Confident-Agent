The Dismantlement of South Africa's Nuclear Weapons
South Africa remains the only country in the world to have built nuclear weapons and then voluntarily given them up. Between 1990 and 1991, the apartheid government dismantled a secretly assembled arsenal of six gun-type nuclear devices and a seventh that was still under construction. The decision, confirmed to the public only in 1993, closed a nuclear weapons programme that had run for roughly two decades and shaped South Africa's later standing as a leading voice for non-proliferation on the African continent.
Origins of the programme
South Africa's nuclear activities began well before any weapons were built. The Atomic Energy Act of 1948 established the Atomic Energy Board to regulate the country's uranium industry, one of the largest in the world. In 1957 South Africa signed a fifty-year cooperation agreement with the United States under the Atoms for Peace programme, which brought the country its first research reactor, SAFARI-1, commissioned at Pelindaba in 1965 and fuelled with highly enriched uranium supplied by the United States.
By 1970 South Africa had moved from civilian nuclear research toward developing its own uranium enrichment capability and, alongside it, a nuclear weapons capacity. The state armaments company Armscor took over the weapons effort from the Atomic Energy Corporation after a planned nuclear test at the Vastrap site in the Kalahari Desert was cancelled in 1977, once Soviet and American satellite imagery alerted the international community to the test preparations. The exposure triggered a mandatory United Nations arms embargo against South Africa later that year. Development continued in secret at a facility west of Pretoria known first as Kentron Circle and later as Advena, where six gun-type nuclear devices were eventually assembled, with a seventh in progress when the programme ended.
The decision to dismantle
The end of the Cold War and the changing regional security picture in southern Africa were central to South Africa's decision to give up its nuclear arsenal. The withdrawal of Cuban forces from Angola, Namibia's move to independence, and the collapse of the Soviet Union removed much of the strategic rationale the apartheid government had used to justify the programme. President F.W. de Klerk, who took office in September 1989, ordered a full review of the nuclear weapons programme shortly afterward. Following a cabinet committee meeting in November 1989, de Klerk decided in principle to terminate the programme, and on 26 February 1990 he issued the written order to dismantle it, setting a target of completion by the end of September 1991.
Historians and former officials have also pointed to South Africa's approaching transition to democracy as a factor in the timing. With the release of Nelson Mandela in February 1990 and negotiations toward majority rule underway, the National Party government faced the prospect of an incoming ANC-led administration inheriting a small nuclear arsenal. Some American and British officials at the time assessed this as a real consideration for Pretoria, though the surviving South African records offer no definitive confirmation, and most participants in the decision denied this motive in later accounts. The dismantlement was substantially complete before the 1994 election.
Dismantling the arsenal
Dismantlement of the six completed weapons and the seventh partially built device took place between 1990 and 1991 under the direction of the Atomic Energy Corporation and Armscor, led by AEC head Waldo Stumpf. Alongside the physical dismantling of the devices, the government destroyed an estimated 12,000 documents relating to the programme, a step later cited as an effort to prevent any successor government from reconstructing the technology, though it has also left significant gaps in the historical record. South Africa acceded to the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT) as a non-nuclear-weapon state in July 1991 and concluded a comprehensive safeguards agreement with the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA), opening its facilities to inspection.
De Klerk's disclosure and IAEA verification
The dismantlement itself was carried out in secret, and South Africa initially hoped to avoid ever publicly admitting the programme had existed. Mounting international scrutiny made that position untenable. On 24 March 1993, de Klerk addressed Parliament and confirmed that South Africa had built and then dismantled a limited nuclear deterrent, while denying any nuclear collaboration with a foreign government. Armscor also disclosed the existence of the Advena facility.
The IAEA subsequently conducted an extensive verification process, with inspection teams working in South Africa between April and August 1993 and continuing technical assessment into 1994. South African officials gave the agency access to thousands of pages of documentation to help reconstruct the history of fissile material production, since the destruction of programme records had complicated any straightforward accounting. On 19 August 1994 the IAEA confirmed that it had verified the dismantlement of the six completed weapons and the partially completed seventh, and concluded it had no indication that any sensitive components of the programme remained that had not been either destroyed or converted to peaceful use. Some independent researchers have continued to question whether every aspect of the programme's history, particularly the full extent of its highly enriched uranium production and any foreign technical assistance, was ever fully disclosed.
Aftermath and non-proliferation legacy
South Africa's disarmament fed directly into a broader regional and international non-proliferation effort. The country joined the Nuclear Suppliers Group in April 1995 and signed the Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty in 1996, ratifying it in 1999. South Africa also played a leading role in negotiating the African Nuclear-Weapon-Free Zone Treaty, known as the Treaty of Pelindaba, opened for signature in Cairo in 1996 and eventually joined by the great majority of African states. Domestically, the 1993 Non-Proliferation of Weapons of Mass Destruction Act formally committed the country to refrain from developing nuclear weapons in future. In February 2019, South Africa ratified the Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons, becoming the first state that had once possessed nuclear weapons, dismantled them, and then joined a treaty banning them outright.
The dismantlement remains a rare case study in voluntary nuclear disarmament and is frequently cited in international discussions of how a state's nuclear rollback might be verified and trusted by the wider world.
